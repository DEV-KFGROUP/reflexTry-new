Aim trainers are easy to misuse. People spray clicks, chase a scoreboard number, then wonder why ranked games still feel sticky. A better approach is a short protocol: accuracy constraints first, speed second, and clear separation between flick and tracking.

## Two skills people blur together

**Flicking** is a discrete jump to a new location—see target, snap, click.  
**Tracking** is continuous pursuit—keep the cursor on a moving target.

Training only flicks will not automatically fix tracking, and vice versa. On Reflextry, use [Flick Shot](/gaming/flick-shot) for snap work and [Aim](/gaming/aim) / [Target Tracking](/gaming/target-tracking) for pursuit.

## A 12-minute protocol

1. **Micro calibration (2 min):** slow precision clicks on small targets ([Precision Click](/accuracy/precision-click) or low-speed aim). Goal: clean hits, no panic spray.  
2. **Flick block (4 min):** medium targets, deliberate resets between shots. Misses count—do not mash.  
3. **Tracking block (4 min):** prioritize time-on-target over flashy flicks.  
4. **Bridge (2 min):** one mixed reflex tool ([Grid](/reflex/grid) or [Reaction](/reflex/reaction)) to wake detection speed, then queue your game.

If you only have six minutes, do steps 1–2.

## Sensitivity and setup notes

Browser aim is not a 1:1 replacement for your in-game sens lab, but consistency still matters:

- Same mouse, same DPI, same browser zoom.  
- Prefer wired mice when logging personal bests.  
- Sit at a stable distance from the display.  
- Disable heavy overlays that steal frames.

Cross-comparing phone touch aim to desktop mouse aim is mostly entertainment.

## What to log

- Hit rate / accuracy  
- Targets destroyed per minute only *after* accuracy is acceptable  
- Subjective “smoothness” (1–5) — surprisingly useful  

A session with slightly lower score but cleaner crosshair paths often transfers better than a chaotic high score.

## Common failure modes

**Spray-and-pray scoring:** high click count, low discipline. Cap yourself: quality over volume.  
**Only easy targets:** ego inflation. Increase difficulty once hit rate is stable.  
**Marathon aim days:** diminishing returns and wrist risk. Prefer daily short blocks.  
**Ignoring fatigue:** if micro-corrections get jagged, stop.

## Transfer expectations

Expect better mouse control and warmup readiness. Do not expect a free rank-up without game-sense, utility, and decision-making practice. Aim is one subsystem.

## Pair with cognitive control

FPS performance also depends on inhibition and attention under distraction. Occasional [Go/No-Go](/reflex/go-no-go) or [Flanker](/reflex/flanker) blocks help you stop panicked peeks and misclicks—not as magic, but as complementary control training. See [Go/No-Go and Flanker Explained](/blog/go-no-go-and-flanker-explained).

## Takeaway

Accuracy constraint → flick block → tracking block → short bridge. Log hit quality. Keep it short. That protocol is usable, honest, and better for long-term improvement than chasing a single vanity number on the hardest setting.

## Wrist and posture (non-negotiable)

Aim practice should not cost you a tendon. Keep the wrist neutral, take micro-breaks every block, and stop at the first sharp pain. Browser toys are optional; injury rehab is not a flex.

If you already have RSI history, prefer shorter blocks and lower click volume. Tracking with smooth paths often loads tissues differently than spam clicking—listen to your body, not the scoreboard.

## Review day once a week

Pick one recorded session (or notes) and ask:

- Did hit rate rise before speed?  
- Did I practice both flick and track?  
- Did I change too many variables (sens, DPI, difficulty) at once?

If the answer to the last question is yes, next week change nothing except consistency. Stable conditions make progress readable.
