People open a reaction test, get a number, and ask: *Is this my true neurological speed?* In a browser, the honest answer is: it is your speed **through this stack**—eyes, brain, hand, device, OS, browser, and page code—on this day.

That stack is still valuable. It is not a sealed laboratory chronoscope. This article draws the line clearly so you can use Reflextry without superstition.

## What a browser trial actually times

In a typical visual simple-reaction trial:

1. The page schedules a stimulus (color change, shape, sound).
2. Your display eventually lights the new pixels (refresh-bound).
3. You detect the change and initiate a motor response.
4. The input device reports the click or key.
5. The browser timestamps the event and computes latency.

Any stage can add milliseconds. A 60 Hz panel updates roughly every 16.7 ms; a 240 Hz panel much faster. Touchscreens and Bluetooth mice often add more latency than a wired gaming mouse. Thermal throttling and background tabs add variance.

## What browser tests are good for

- **Personal baselines** on the same machine and difficulty.
- **Warmup feedback** before ranked games or practice.
- **Habit building:** short daily blocks beat rare marathon sessions.
- **Comparing task types** on the same device (simple vs choice, Go vs No-Go).
- **Learning clean technique:** fewer early clicks, steadier averages.

If your average simple RT drops from 280 ms to 230 ms over three weeks on the same laptop, that is a real practice signal for *you*, even if a lab might print a slightly different absolute number.

## What they are not

- **Not a medical diagnosis.** Fatigue, caffeine, mood, sleep, and illness move scores. Tools do not replace clinicians.
- **Not certified occupational screens** (aviation color vision, clinical neuropsychology batteries, etc.).
- **Not population IQ claims.** We refuse invented percentiles from uncontrolled web traffic.
- **Not cross-device leaderboard truth.** Phone vs desktop is a different experiment.

## How to make your numbers more trustworthy

1. Use one primary device for tracking.
2. Close heavy tabs; disable night filters if color/timing matters.
3. Prefer wired mice for reaction and aim tools when possible.
4. Warm up 1–2 throwaway blocks before logging a “real” average.
5. Record difficulty mode beside every personal best.
6. Prefer multi-trial averages over a single lucky click.

## Sound and peripheral caveats

Auditory cues depend on audio stack latency and output device. Peripheral-vision tasks depend on viewing distance and screen size. Treat those scores as within-setup metrics, not universal physiology.

## How Reflextry designs for honesty

We show transparent units (ms, spans, accuracy, completion time). Difficulty changes the task—so Beginner and Professional are not the same test. Methodology write-ups live in the [Cognitive Journal](/blog), including [How We Score Reflextry Tests](/blog/how-we-score-reflextry-tests).

If something looks broken (impossible sub-100 ms averages on a 60 Hz panel with early-click spam), trust the error counters and your setup notes before trusting the ego number.

## A sensible weekly use pattern

- **2–4 days/week:** 10–15 minutes across reaction + one memory or accuracy tool.
- **Before competitive play:** 5-minute simple + aim or grid warmup.
- **Monthly:** reread your notes; change only one variable at a time (sleep, device, difficulty).

## Bottom line

Browser reaction tests measure a real skill loop under real-world noise. Use them to train and track. Do not treat them as courtroom evidence about your nervous system.
