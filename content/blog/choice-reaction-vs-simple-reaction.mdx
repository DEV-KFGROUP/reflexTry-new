Simple reaction time asks one question: *when* did the signal appear? Choice reaction asks two: *when* and *which* response is correct? Mixing those scores is one of the most common mistakes people make when they compare “my RT” across apps and studies.

This guide explains the difference in plain language, why more alternatives usually cost milliseconds, and how to practice each task on Reflextry without false comparisons.

## Simple reaction in one sentence

A cue appears (screen turns green, a tone plays, a target flashes). You respond as soon as you detect it. There is typically one legal action. Early responses before the cue are errors—anticipation, not speed.

On Reflextry, the classic green-screen reaction test is the simple-reaction family. Your primary number is average latency in milliseconds on valid trials.

## Choice reaction in one sentence

Several possible stimuli map to several responses. You must identify *which* option appeared, then act. Wrong-box clicks and premature clicks should not inflate a “fast” average; they belong in an error count.

Reflextry’s choice-reaction tool lights one of several colored targets. Average RT on correct hits is the speed metric; accuracy is the quality metric. Both matter.

## Why choice is usually slower

Cognitive psychology has long observed that decision time tends to rise as the number of equally likely alternatives increases (often discussed under Hick’s law). You do not need the formula to train well. Intuition is enough:

- Detecting “something changed” is cheaper than classifying *what* changed.
- More mappings mean more chances to pick the wrong action under time pressure.
- Practice reduces that cost, but it does not make a 4-choice task identical to a 1-choice task.

So a 190 ms simple RT and a 260 ms 4-choice RT can both be strong for their own tasks. They are not interchangeable bragging rights.

## Hardware still matters

Browser tests inherit display refresh, input device latency, and background load. That noise affects both simple and choice tasks. Keep comparisons **within the same device, same difficulty, and same number of alternatives**.

If you upgrade from a 60 Hz laptop trackpad to a 240 Hz monitor and a gaming mouse, expect scores to move. Document the setup next to personal bests.

## How to train without sabotaging yourself

**Week pattern that works:**

1. **Baseline day:** 2–3 short simple-reaction blocks, then 2–3 choice blocks at the same difficulty. Write averages and error counts.
2. **Simple focus days:** emphasize clean detection—no anticipating the flash. If early clicks rise, you are gambling, not reacting.
3. **Choice focus days:** slow enough to keep accuracy high, then shave time. A fast wrong click is not progress.
4. **Mixed warmup (5–8 minutes):** one simple block, one choice block before gaming or sport practice.

Stop a session when errors spike or you feel rushed. Fatigue masquerades as “getting slower,” which leads people to grind longer and learn worse habits.

## Reading Reflextry numbers

- **Simple reaction:** lower average ms is better; watch early-click rate.
- **Choice reaction:** lower average ms *on corrects* is better; track wrong-target rate separately.
- **Difficulty modes:** treat Beginner and Professional as separate leaderboards in your notes.

## Common myths

**“I should only care about the lowest single trial.”** Outliers happen. Prefer block averages and stability across days.

**“Choice RT should match simple RT if I’m elite.”** Elite performers are fast at both, but choice still carries decision cost. Train both.

**“More caffeine always equals better RT.”** It can help alertness until jitter and early clicks rise. Measure, don’t assume.

## Practical takeaway

Use simple reaction to train detection speed. Use choice reaction to train classification under time pressure. Log them separately. Short, honest sessions beat long sessions that reward guessing.
