Go/No-Go looks simple: press on Go, withhold on No-Go. The metric people stare at first is Go reaction time. The metric that actually tells you whether you learned the task is often the **false alarm**—pressing when you should have stayed still.

## The two error types

- **Miss (omission):** Go appeared; you failed to press in time.  
- **False alarm (commission):** No-Go appeared; you pressed anyway.

A flashy Go RT with a pile of false alarms means you are anticipating and gambling. That can feel fast. It is not controlled performance.

## Why false alarms rise

- Predominant Go trials create a “press bias.”  
- Fatigue and distraction reduce brakes more than engines.  
- Speed goals without accuracy constraints reward impulsivity.  
- Stimuli that look alike until late in the flash window invite late corrections that arrive as presses.

## How to train the brake

1. **Start with accuracy goals.** Example: keep false alarms under a personal threshold before chasing ms.  
2. **Use short blocks.** Inhibition collapses when you grind angry.  
3. **Breathe on No-Go.** A deliberate non-response is a skill rep.  
4. **Alternate with Flanker.** Conflict tasks also punish impulsive center-ignoring responses—see [Go/No-Go and Flanker Explained](/blog/go-no-go-and-flanker-explained).  
5. **Re-check simple reaction separately.** Detection speed and inhibition are related but not identical—[Choice vs Simple Reaction](/blog/choice-reaction-vs-simple-reaction).

## Reading Reflextry Go/No-Go

Treat the session as a pair: Go RT on correct hits **and** false-alarm rate. Improvement is:

- Stable or better withhold accuracy, then  
- Lower Go RT without sacrificing that accuracy.

Difficulty modes that increase No-Go frequency or tighten timing change the experiment—log the mode.

## Real-world analogy (without overclaiming)

Driving, ranked shooters, and typing all punish impulsive actions: lane changes without checks, pre-firing, sending before proofreading. Go/No-Go is a clean lab toy for that brake. It is not a personality diagnosis.

## A 10-minute inhibition micro-session

- 4 min Go/No-Go — accuracy primary  
- 3 min Flanker — congruent vs incongruent awareness  
- 3 min simple reaction — clean detection, minimal early clicks  

Stop if false alarms spike. Come back tomorrow.

## Takeaway

Fast Go with loud false alarms is not mastery. Train the withhold, then shave time. Practice on [Go/No-Go](/reflex/go-no-go) and read scoring context in [How We Score Reflextry Tests](/blog/how-we-score-reflextry-tests).

## A note on “predatory Go” designs

Some casual games make almost every trial a Go and sprinkle rare No-Gos. That design creates a strong press habit, which is educational—but it also means your first sessions will look ugly. Expect false alarms early. The learning curve is precisely the brake catching up to the engine.

If a mode feels unfairly Go-heavy, lean into it as brake practice rather than rage-quitting. Log false-alarm rate as your primary KPI for a week, then reintroduce speed goals.

## Pairing with sleep and stimulants

Sleep debt raises commission errors for many people. Extra caffeine can help alertness until tremor and anticipation create early presses. If your false-alarm chart spikes after a late night, believe the chart: recover sleep before blaming the tool.

## Sharing scores with a coach or teammate

When you share a Go/No-Go result, include: difficulty, Go RT average, false-alarm count or rate, miss count, device, and time of day. A lone “I got 210 ms” screenshot without withhold stats is incomplete—and coaches should ask for the missing half.
